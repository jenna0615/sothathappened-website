Blinded by the Fog: The Day a CB Radio Rumor Almost Wrecked Me

True Stories | 1990s Trucking

Picture this: It’s the early 1990s. No cell phones, no social media. I’m in my early twenties, I’ve only been a truck driver for a few months, and there are very few women behind the wheel of an eighteen-wheeler.

My boyfriend and I are team-driving through Southern California on one of the foggiest mornings I have ever seen. Visibility is maybe forty yards. I’ve just loaded up a haul of fresh produce, and to get to my next stop, I have to drive my rig across the double lane Highway 86. To make things extra spicy… my logbook isn't up to date. (For those of you who don’t know what a log book is: truck drivers use this to keep a record for hours of service and compliance with regulations for on-duty and off-duty times.)

I creep up to the intersection. The median ahead is incredibly skinny—just big enough for my tractor, but not the trailer. If I try to cross and get stuck, my trailer will be blocking the road. I roll the windows down just to listen for engines. Nothing. I sit there for what feels like ages, completely paralyzed by fear. Finally, I wake up my boyfriend. He pops up to the front passenger seat and leans out his window listening for southbound traffic; I listen for northbound.

I take a breath, and I make the plunge.

Sure enough, my tractor hits the median, we hear southbound traffic coming, and I have to stomp on the brakes. Just like that, my worst nightmare becomes reality: my trailer is planted squarely across both northbound lanes.

Then, out of the blinding fog, comes the sound of screeching tires.

Through the haze, I glimpse a beverage delivery truck—you know, the ones with the side-loading doors. The driver wildly maneuvers around the back of my trailer. Missed us! No impact. I send a quick thank you to the universe for that driver’s situational awareness.

But the relief vanishes instantly. Because the screeching tires aren’t stopping. BANG.

An old-style seventies van slams directly into my trailer, embedding itself all the way up to the windshield. Another car barely scoots around the wreckage. Within seconds, a quiet highway turns into a disaster zone.

I throw the truck in park, pop the brakes (that’s a truck driving thing), jump out, tell my boyfriend to stay put, and run into the fog toward the van. Two elderly gentlemen are in the front seats. Thankfully, they’re uninjured, just in shock. The driver shakes it off, tells me he thinks he’s ok, and backs up out from underneath my trailer. We both know we're still in a terrifyingly dangerous spot with an eighteen-wheeler blocking the highway. I tell him I'm moving the truck to the shoulder, and right then… the universe smiles on us. A California Highway Patrol car materializes out of the fog like an angel.

Except, my relief immediately turns into pure panic.

Back then, the rumor mill on the CB radio was clear: 'California cops hate truck drivers.' I’m a twenty-something kid standing in the middle of a foggy highway with a blocked road, a fresh accident, and an un-logged day. I honestly think I'm going to jail.

The officer takes control, calls for backup, and tells me to get my rig safely across onto the shoulder. I jump into the cab, get the rig across the southbound lanes and onto the shoulder of the road. I grab my logbook, and start frantically trying to make entries for the day. Then I stop myself. What am I doing? There's just been an accident and I'm stressing over a piece of paper? That’s what the CB radio rumors will do to your head. I throw the book down and walk back.

By now, backup has arrived. Officer Number One—a female cop—tells me the gentlemen in the van are okay, and then says the words that make my stomach drop: Do you mind if we head over to my car? It’s parked behind your truck. You can grab your license, registration, permit book, and logbook and meet me there.

This is it, I think. Handcuffs.

She has me sit in the passenger seat of her cruiser. I hand her my driver’s license, my permit book (which has proof of insurance and registration inside) and, yes, my log book with my un-logged day. She then asked me what happened and told her the absolute truth from the moment I left the produce house. While I was speaking, she was writing down information from my DL and my permit book.

I finish talking, bracing for the worst. She looks at me, pauses, and says: This is a clear case of you having possession of the intersection. Oncoming traffic had to yield to you. The driver of the van was going seventy in a fifty-five. He hit you at forty-five miles an hour because he was speeding in a blind fog. He’s lucky to be alive. Plus, he has no insurance.

She closes her notebook. And she never even glances at my logbook.

Then she smiles and says: He deserves to go to jail for this, but it’s Friday, and I go on maternity leave tomorrow. So he’s extra lucky today.

When I tell you that wave of absolute relief washed over me, I mean it—she didn’t even look at my logbook! And hindsight is real. The fact that rumor had me so worked up that I was more worried about a logbook and jail than I was anything else is telling about how impactful rumor can be.

I’m in my 50s now, and my big-rig days are safely parked in the past, but the events of that fateful, incredibly foggy day in Southern California are still burned into my brain in high-definition. I can still see it perfectly: the wrecked van, the face of the female officer, the driver of the van, and the first cop who rolled up to the scene. It was a huge moment for me, mostly because it completely flipped how I viewed the entire world.

Instead of the nightmare I expected, that interaction taught me a lesson I’ve carried into my adult life ever since. It forced me to hit the brakes on gossip, reject exaggerated second-hand rumors, and realize that every single person speaks from their own unique reality and perspective. Getting that reality check early on turned me into an independent thinker who looks for the facts instead of riding the rumor mill.

If you’ve made it this far, I encourage you to hit your own brakes and listen with true objectivity. Step outside your own perspective and consider how a situation genuinely impacts the other person, rather than how it affects you. By pulling back, removing narrow-minded thinking, and looking at the scene from the outside, you might just find a better way to help them navigate their own road ahead.
