Blinded by the Fog: The Day a CB Radio Rumor Almost Wrecked Me

1990s Trucking | True Stories

Picture this: It’s the early 1990s. No cell phones, no social media. I’m in my early twenties, I’ve only been a truck driver for a few months, and there are very few women behind the wheel of an eighteen-wheeler.

My boyfriend and I are team-driving through Southern California on one of the foggiest mornings I have ever seen. Visibility is maybe forty yards. I’ve just loaded up a haul of fresh produce, and to get to my next stop, I have to drive my rig across the double lane Highway 86. To make things extra spicy, my logbook isn't up to date...

As the dense white soup blanked out the highway markers, every crackle on the CB radio made my nerves tighter. The rumor mill was spinning tales about severe highway checks ahead, and there I was with a messy logbook and an 80,000-pound rig to navigate across lanes I could barely see.

When the van finally emerged from the gray haze, time slowed down completely. But the true surprise wasn't the near-miss collision itself—it was the profound realization that everything the gossip channels had fed me was an absolute lie. That chaotic morning became the definitive turning point that completely changed how I viewed the world, forcing me to reject second-hand rumors and look strictly for the objective facts.

One Angel After Another: A True Story of Purpose

2000s Medical Miracle | True Stories

I shouldn’t be here telling you this story. By all medical logic, my time should have run out decades ago. But the universe had other plans for me. Today, I want to tell you about the why, and the miraculous how.

In September of 2001, our entire nation was reeling. We had just experienced one of the most heartbreaking events in modern history with the tragic loss of life at the Twin Towers. As fate would have it, my parents happened to be visiting me from out of state during that exact time.

Then came the defining moment of the entire ordeal. My nurse walked over holding an official clipboard with another document for me to sign. It was an authorization form giving the team permission to perform an emergency blood transfusion, because my counts had tanked to a dangerous low. I looked at it, took a breath, and said, "No. I refuse."

Now, before you judge that decision, understand that this was not a religious choice, nor was it cultural. I was completely calm, entirely coherent, and at total peace with whatever was about to happen to me. But on a deep, soul level, I knew with absolute certainty that I did not want another person's blood inside me. The shock on her face was real, as it was for every other nurse in that room. She stood at my bedside and explained in no uncertain terms that if I refused the transfusion, I could very well die from blood loss on the operating table. I held my ground firmly. "I understand the consequences, and I am okay with whatever happens. But I am still not going to sign." And I meant it.

By the time they wheeled me into the operating room, I was fading in and out of consciousness. It was no easy job for the surgical team, but that anesthesiologist and my surgeon took up the mantle of heroes. The surgery went flawlessly. When I finally opened my eyes in the recovery room, Diane was the first thing I saw. She had never left. Her care over the next two months saved me. During my stay, her German Shepherd gave birth to nine puppies, and I was gifted the firstborn, Max—another angel sent to guide me.

It wasn’t a destiny meant to be defined by one massive, singular achievement. Instead, my purpose is woven into the everyday moments of human connection. Today, I have a beautiful daughter, and I am constantly, purposefully placed in situations where I can have a direct, positive, and beneficial impact on the lives of others. People come and go from our lives like passing shadows, but I have learned never to question the timing. If our pathways cross in this life, I know it is for a divine reason. Every trial we survive is simply preparation for the people we are meant to help heal down the road. I am here for it. I am here for all of it.

Blinded by the Fog: The Day a CB Radio Rumor Almost Wrecked Me

True Stories | 1990s Trucking

Picture this: It’s the early 1990s. No cell phones, no social media. I’m in my early twenties, I’ve only been a truck driver for a few months, and there are very few women behind the wheel of an eighteen-wheeler.

My boyfriend and I are team-driving through Southern California on one of the foggiest mornings I have ever seen. Visibility is maybe forty yards. I’ve just loaded up a haul of fresh produce, and to get to my next stop, I have to drive my rig across the double lane Highway 86. To make things extra spicy… my logbook isn't up to date. (For those of you who don’t know what a log book is: truck drivers use this to keep a record for hours of service and compliance with regulations for on-duty and off-duty times.)

I creep up to the intersection. The median ahead is incredibly skinny—just big enough for my tractor, but not the trailer. If I try to cross and get stuck, my trailer will be blocking the road. I roll the windows down just to listen for engines. Nothing. I sit there for what feels like ages, completely paralyzed by fear. Finally, I wake up my boyfriend. He pops up to the front passenger seat and leans out his window listening for southbound traffic; I listen for northbound.

I take a breath, and I make the plunge.

Sure enough, my tractor hits the median, we hear southbound traffic coming, and I have to stomp on the brakes. Just like that, my worst nightmare becomes reality: my trailer is planted squarely across both northbound lanes.

Then, out of the blinding fog, comes the sound of screeching tires.

Through the haze, I glimpse a beverage delivery truck—you know, the ones with the side-loading doors. The driver wildly maneuvers around the back of my trailer. Missed us! No impact. I send a quick thank you to the universe for that driver’s situational awareness.

But the relief vanishes instantly. Because the screeching tires aren’t stopping. BANG.

An old-style seventies van slams directly into my trailer, embedding itself all the way up to the windshield. Another car barely scoots around the wreckage. Within seconds, a quiet highway turns into a disaster zone.

I throw the truck in park, pop the brakes (that’s a truck driving thing), jump out, tell my boyfriend to stay put, and run into the fog toward the van. Two elderly gentlemen are in the front seats. Thankfully, they’re uninjured, just in shock. The driver shakes it off, tells me he thinks he’s ok, and backs up out from underneath my trailer. We both know we're still in a terrifyingly dangerous spot with an eighteen-wheeler blocking the highway. I tell him I'm moving the truck to the shoulder, and right then… the universe smiles on us. A California Highway Patrol car materializes out of the fog like an angel.

Except, my relief immediately turns into pure panic.

Back then, the rumor mill on the CB radio was clear: 'California cops hate truck drivers.' I’m a twenty-something kid standing in the middle of a foggy highway with a blocked road, a fresh accident, and an un-logged day. I honestly think I'm going to jail.

The officer takes control, calls for backup, and tells me to get my rig safely across onto the shoulder. I jump into the cab, get the rig across the southbound lanes and onto the shoulder of the road. I grab my logbook, and start frantically trying to make entries for the day. Then I stop myself. What am I doing? There's just been an accident and I'm stressing over a piece of paper? That’s what the CB radio rumors will do to your head. I throw the book down and walk back.

By now, backup has arrived. Officer Number One—a female cop—tells me the gentlemen in the van are okay, and then says the words that make my stomach drop: Do you mind if we head over to my car? It’s parked behind your truck. You can grab your license, registration, permit book, and logbook and meet me there.

This is it, I think. Handcuffs.

She has me sit in the passenger seat of her cruiser. I hand her my driver’s license, my permit book (which has proof of insurance and registration inside) and, yes, my log book with my un-logged day. She then asked me what happened and told her the absolute truth from the moment I left the produce house. While I was speaking, she was writing down information from my DL and my permit book.

I finish talking, bracing for the worst. She looks at me, pauses, and says: This is a clear case of you having possession of the intersection. Oncoming traffic had to yield to you. The driver of the van was going seventy in a fifty-five. He hit you at forty-five miles an hour because he was speeding in a blind fog. He’s lucky to be alive. Plus, he has no insurance.

She closes her notebook. And she never even glances at my logbook.

Then she smiles and says: He deserves to go to jail for this, but it’s Friday, and I go on maternity leave tomorrow. So he’s extra lucky today.

When I tell you that wave of absolute relief washed over me, I mean it—she didn’t even look at my logbook! And hindsight is real. The fact that rumor had me so worked up that I was more worried about a logbook and jail than I was anything else is telling about how impactful rumor can be.

I’m in my 50s now, and my big-rig days are safely parked in the past, but the events of that fateful, incredibly foggy day in Southern California are still burned into my brain in high-definition. I can still see it perfectly: the wrecked van, the face of the female officer, the driver of the van, and the first cop who rolled up to the scene. It was a huge moment for me, mostly because it completely flipped how I viewed the entire world.

Instead of the nightmare I expected, that interaction taught me a lesson I’ve carried into my adult life ever since. It forced me to hit the brakes on gossip, reject exaggerated second-hand rumors, and realize that every single person speaks from their own unique reality and perspective. Getting that reality check early on turned me into an independent thinker who looks for the facts instead of riding the rumor mill.

If you’ve made it this far, I encourage you to hit your own brakes and listen with true objectivity. Step outside your own perspective and consider how a situation genuinely impacts the other person, rather than how it affects you. By pulling back, removing narrow-minded thinking, and looking at the scene from the outside, you might just find a better way to help them navigate their own road ahead.


One Angel After Another: A True Story of Purpose

True Stories | 2000s Medical Miracle

I shouldn’t be here telling you this story. By all medical logic, my time should have run out decades ago. But the universe had other plans for me. Today, I want to tell you about the why, and the miraculous how.

In September of 2001, our entire nation was reeling. We had just experienced one of the most heartbreaking events in modern history with the tragic loss of life at the Twin Towers. As fate would have it, my parents happened to be visiting me from out of state during that exact time. I am forever grateful they were there. Together, we sorted through the heavy emotions of that week with a lot of tears, tight hugs, and shared comfort.

By the end of September, my parents were safely back home, and it was time for my routine yearly checkup. I was in my mid-twenties. As my fellow female listeners will agree, these appointments are always unpleasant, but usually nothing to worry about.

That changed in early October. I was at work when an urgent message came through. My doctor’s office had been trying to reach me at home, but my landline was busy, so they called my employer. Keep in mind, this was back in the day when cell phones were just starting to hit the market—the kind of tech you needed serious money to own, and I wasn't there yet. On my break, I called them back. That was the moment I received the news no woman ever wants to hear, especially at twenty-eight years old: "You have Stage 1 Cervical Cancer."

The doctor explained that this was highly uncommon for someone my age. However, they were optimistic. If we treated it immediately, the likelihood of eradicating it completely was incredibly high. And so, my journey to the operating room began. I notified my job, took the necessary time off, and underwent surgery to remove the cancerous tissue. After six days of strict rest, I felt strong enough to return to work on the seventh day post-surgery.

And that seventh day is when it all fell apart. Halfway through my first day back, I was standing outside my supervisor’s office, waiting to catch her as soon as she finished a phone call. Suddenly, I felt a rush of something warm and hot. It felt exactly like I was urinating, but I knew I wasn’t. Panic flashed through my mind: Oh dear, am I bleeding?

I waved my arm wildly to get my supervisor’s attention, turned around, and pointed to my backside. When I turned back to look at her, she looked like a ghost. The blood had completely drained from her face. She slammed the phone down onto the receiver. "That’s blood! Should I call an ambulance?"

Young and naive, I completely shrugged off the gravity of what was happening. This can't be that serious, I thought. It's fine. "No, let me just head to the restroom and see what’s going on," I told her. But standing in that restroom stall, visually witnessing the shocking amount of blood leaving my body, the reality set in. Yet, I remained strangely calm. Only slightly worried, I wadded up a massive amount of toilet paper, waddled back out to my desk, grabbed my purse, and picked up my keys. I walked past my supervisor’s door and calmly told her I was driving myself to the emergency room, just in case.

She picked up the phone again, her voice tight. "That’s a terrible idea. Let me call an ambulance right now!" "No, I’m good," I replied. "It’ll take more time for them to get here and get me out to the rig than it will if I just drive." And with that, I walked out to my car.

About halfway to the hospital, a strange moment of grace occurred. An ambulance pulled up right next to me at a red light. The paramedic in the passenger seat looked out her window, glanced at me, and did a literal double-take—then a triple-take. She quickly rolled down her window. Mine was already down; it was a sweltering hot day, and my car didn’t have air conditioning. She pointed at my car seat and shouted, "Are you okay?!"

Call it protective denial, but up to that exact moment, I had refused to look down. As I was driving, I could feel the energy draining out of my body. It is a terrifying sensation that I can’t fully put into words, but on a primal level, I knew something catastrophic was happening. I was choosing not to look on purpose. Sitting in that car, I was firmly clenching my legs together, hoping the pressure would slow the flow. It wasn't working. When I finally looked down, the puddle of blood in my lap wasn't just a puddle anymore. It was a lake, and it was about to overflow onto the seat.

I looked back at the paramedic. "I think so. I’m heading to the hospital right now!" "Follow us!" she commanded. She snapped her head back toward her partner, and off we went. There were no lights or sirens, which I’m sure would have been against regulations without a patient in the back, but they channeled through those city streets faster than I thought possible. Before I knew it, I was pulling up to the emergency room entrance. The paramedic was already out, waving me into a parking spot while her partner sprinted inside.

He emerged seconds later pushing a wheelchair, while she ran over with armfuls of towels and gauze to stem the bleeding. They rushed me through the doors, right past a packed waiting room, and straight into a trauma bay. Immediately, a nurse began hammering me with a dozen rapid-fire questions: who I was, what happened, my medical history. I gave them every piece of information I had. I told them about the cold knife incision surgery from a week prior, my doctor's name, everything. A flurry of medical staff moved around my bed, a chaotic storm of motion, but the nurse getting my story was extraordinary. She managed to hold my attention so completely that the chaos faded into the background. She was the calm in the eye of the storm. An angel. My angel.

While I was answering questions, a young phlebotomist came in to start an IV. She was clearly brand new and nervously admitted it to me. Because of the massive blood loss I had already sustained, she struggled to find a viable vein in the back of my left hand. Her hands were steady, but she was nervous. Despite my own intense dislike of needles, I found myself trying to soothe her. I looked at her and said, "It's okay. Just take a breath and do your thing." Eventually, she got the line in.

Once the initial rush of questions slowed down, I finally took stock of the room. I noticed a nurse frantically trying to untangle a cluster of long, clear hoses attached to the wall. I later learned this was a specialized medical vacuum designed to clear blood away from the surgical site so the doctor could actually see the source of the hemorrhage. Right then, the emergency physician burst into the room. Dr. Wigger. I will never forget his name, because his energy perfectly matched the tension in the air. He was a very intense, high-strung man. Seeing that the vacuum lines weren't ready, his frustration boiled over. He snatched the tangled hoses right out of the nurse’s hands and began aggressively ripping through the knots himself.

Lying there, knowing I was actively bleeding out from one of the most vulnerable and private parts of my body, the last thing I wanted to experience was an angry, stressed-out doctor preparing for an invasive emergency exam. I needed whoever was handling my life in that moment to be nice, calm, and steady. His energy was the exact opposite. So, I reached out my left hand, placed it gently on his forearm, and looked him in the eye. "Doctor, it'll be okay. They’re just some hoses."

It was an immediate about-face. The tension left his shoulders, and he instantly slid into the exact calm, focused state I needed. I will forever be grateful to him for recognizing what I required in that terrifying moment and adjusting his course. The exam went exactly as you would expect. He discovered a significant tear in the newly formed scar tissue from my cancer surgery. I needed to go back under the knife immediately to cauterize the hemorrhaging vessel. Oh, the irony—the first surgery was a cold knife, and the emergency fix was fire. But the universe threw in a bonus: my original surgeon happened to be upstairs in the hospital delivering a baby, and he was able to rush down to perform the procedure.

Progress was being made, but my body was deteriorating fast. Mass blood loss has a way of turning out the lights. As they prepped the room, my angel nurse walked over, laid her hand gently on my arm, and looked at me with pure amazement. "I’ve been a nurse for over twenty-five years," she whispered. "And that is the first time I have ever seen a patient calm the doctor down. It’s always the other way around." I smiled and explained why I did it, and right there in the middle of a trauma room, we both shared a beautiful, heavy chuckle.

That chuckle, however, launched me straight into hyperventilation. My body was simply out of fuel. The nurse went old-school—she grabbed a brown paper bag, held it over my face, and told me to breathe into it. Instant relief. I held onto that bag like a lifeline, using it every time I felt the panic creeping back in.

Then came the defining moment of the entire ordeal.

My nurse walked over holding an official clipboard with another document for me to sign. It was an authorization form giving the team permission to perform an emergency blood transfusion, because my counts had tanked to a dangerous low. I looked at it, took a breath, and said, "No. I refuse."

Now, before you judge that decision, understand that this was not a religious choice, nor was it cultural. I was completely calm, entirely coherent, and at total peace with whatever was about to happen to me. But on a deep, soul level, I knew with absolute certainty that I did not want another person's blood inside me.

The shock on her face was real, as it was for every other nurse in that room. She did her due diligence. She stood at my bedside and explained in no uncertain terms that if I refused the transfusion, I could very well die from blood loss on the operating table. I looked back at her and held my ground. "I understand the consequences, and I am okay with whatever happens. But I am still not going to sign." And I meant it. I was completely at peace with the thought that the next few hours might be my last on this earth.

As they finally wheeled my gurney out of the trauma bay and up toward the pre-op suite, I overheard my angel nurse turn to one of her colleagues. She said, "I’ve never met anyone like her before. But I know she’s going to be just fine." I held onto those words tightly.

In pre-op, I was met by a wonderfully charming anesthesiologist. He took one look at my left hand and immediately realized the rookie phlebotomist had botched the IV line; the fluid was rushing into the surrounding tissue instead of the vein. He adjusted something on the hose line that I couldn't see, and the sudden, intense pressure nearly made me leap straight out of the hospital bed. It hurt terribly. "Well, that’s certainly not supposed to happen if it’s in there properly," he said with a disarming, brilliant smile that instantly made me forget the pain. He set to work redoing the line, placing it safely into the crook of my arm rather than my badly bruised hand.

During this time, two of my colleagues from work—who were also very close friends—burst into the room after hearing what had happened. They stayed by my side until I was wheeled into the operating room, lovingly chastising me the entire time. "How could you be so reckless?! Why won't you take the blood?!" It was all said out of pure love, and I held absolutely no ill feelings toward them. I simply held my ground firmly. In fact, one of those friends, Diane, would prove to be the ultimate catalyst for my survival.

By the time they wheeled me into the operating room, I was fading in and out of consciousness. It was no easy job for the surgical team, but that anesthesiologist and my surgeon took up the mantle of heroes. The surgery went flawlessly. When I finally opened my eyes in the recovery room, Diane was the first thing I saw. She had never left.

When the doctor came in to report on the surgery, his expression was a mix of awe and gravity. He looked at me and said, "You are a living miracle. By all medical standards, given the sheer volume of blood you lost, you shouldn't even be awake right now, let alone surviving." But the road ahead would be grueling. My recovery would take months, not days. My iron counts were completely tanked, and because my immune system was practically non-existent, catching even a minor cold could literally mean certain death. It was grave news, but I just rolled with it. I was alive. And that was everything.

At the time, I was single and lived completely alone. I had no family living in the area and only my friends to rely on for post-operative care. Diane didn't hesitate for a single second. She loaded me up into her family van and took me straight to her home. Diane was managing a chaotic household—she had three kids, two massive German Shepherds, and her husband was currently deployed overseas with the military. Yet, she and her two oldest children wrapped their lives around my recovery. They prepared specialized, nutrient-dense meals to rebuild my blood counts. They literally helped me eat, because for the first week, I didn't even have the physical strength to lift a fork to my mouth. They helped me get dressed, assisted me to the bathroom, and ran all of my errands to keep my bills paid. Because she had heard the doctor's warnings about my nonexistent immune system, Diane disinfected her entire house from top to bottom every single day. What an absolutely phenomenal woman.

During my two-month stay at her house—and yes, it took a full two months just for me to gain the strength to walk for longer than five minutes at a time—Diane's German Shepherd gave birth to a litter of nine puppies. I was gifted the firstborn of the litter. That dog, Max, became yet another angel sent to guide me through life. But that is a beautiful story for another day. Looking back, it was one angel after another. One life-altering crossroads after another. And through it all, I am still here; here and healthy. At the time, lying in that hospital bed and recovering in Diane’s home, I knew deep in my soul that I was being preserved for a distinct destiny. I just didn't know what it was yet. I do now.

It wasn’t a destiny meant to be defined by one massive, singular achievement. Instead, my purpose is woven into the everyday moments of human connection. Today, I have a beautiful daughter, and I am constantly, purposefully placed in situations where I can have a direct, positive, and beneficial impact on the lives of others. People come and go from our lives like passing shadows, but I have learned never to question the timing. If our pathways cross in this life, I know it is for a divine reason. Every trial we survive is simply preparation for the people we are meant to help heal down the road. I am here for it. I am here for all of it.
